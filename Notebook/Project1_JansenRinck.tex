\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}}

Almost everyone experienced something out of the ordinary during the
onset of the 2020 pandemic, and Montana's waterways were no exception.
Montana saw approximately a 28\% increase in angling pressure across the
state, but where did those increases happen? That is what I will explore
through this geospatial analysis. I hypothesized that this increase did
not impact rivers uniformly, but concentrated to rivers that were less
popular pre-pandemic. Data collected by Fish, Wildlife, and Parks from
the angling surveys covers the fishing license year (March -- February);
I will be looking at license years 2019 and 2020 for this analysis. Data
brought in from the Montana State Library was in the Coordinate
Reference System (CRS) EPSG:6514, projected to NAD83(2011)/Montana State
Plane; all layers were exported and reprojected to this CRS and
projection system for this analysis.

FWP's angling pressure metric is calculated using the following:

\[
P_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{E_{ij}}{R_{ij}} \cdot D_{ij} \cdot A_{ij}
\]

where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(P_j\) = pressure for an individual water by the \(j\)th residency
\item
  \(E_{ij}\) = number of eligible anglers for the \(i\)th wave and
  \(j\)th residency
\item
  \(D_{ij}\) = days fished on that particular water for the \(i\)th wave
  and \(j\)th residency
\item
  \(R_{ij}\) = number of respondents from the survey for the \(i\)th
  wave and \(j\)th residency
\item
  \(A_{ij}\) = adjustment factor for non-response for the \(i\)th wave
  and \(j\)th residency
\item
  \(n\) = number of waves in the estimate year or season
\item
  \(j\) = residency type (resident, nonresident, or total)
\end{itemize}

For this analysis, I combined the \(P_j\) (resident) and \(P_j\)
(non-resident) metrics to examine overall fishing pressure for the state
of Montana.

    \hypertarget{original-hypothesis}{%
\subsection{Original Hypothesis:}\label{original-hypothesis}}

During the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, angling pressure from
residents and non-residents became more dispersed across rivers, with
angling pressure increasing on less-popular rivers compared to 2019.

    \hypertarget{refined-research-question}{%
\subsection{Refined Research Question}\label{refined-research-question}}

Did 2020 angling pressure increases concentrate on rivers that were
already popular in 2019, independent of their distance from population
centers?

This first view examines how angling pressure changed per fishing access

    \hypertarget{data-processing}{%
\subsubsection{Data Processing}\label{data-processing}}

To normalize Montana streams by FWP fishing access points, I first had
to filter the raw access site data down to points actually located along
the rivers examined in this analysis, since the dataset also includes
points on lakes and reservoirs. Matching each point to its corresponding
river turned out to be difficult - the access point data didn't have a
field that specified what river or waterbody each point lies on, so I
resolved this by classifying each point by proximity, comparing its
distance to the nearest river. Once that data was classified and parsed,
I used the ``Statistics by Category'' tool to group the points by
individual river and get counts for each. With the access point count
joined back onto the stream data I appended the count as a new attribute
based on the shared river name field - I use this same join method
throughout this analysis. I calculated angling days per access point for
each river in 2019 and 2020, then found the percent change between the
two years. Looking at the resulting percentages, it was clear there was
a steep drop-off in pressure metrics for rivers that had fewer than six
access points, so I excluded rivers below that threshold for this part
of the analysis.

Based on the results above, it doesn't look like my original hypothesis
held true. Rivers farther from population centers don't appear to have
received a disproportionate amount of angling pressure compared to
rivers closer to dense populations. In fact, it looks like the opposite
in places - the Bitterroot, Blackfoot, Gallatin, and Big Hole all sit
near Missoula and Bozeman and all show large per-mile increases. This
pattern of geographic clustering in southwestern Montana would lead me
to believe that lockdown measures kept people closer to home and anglers
didn't travel as much in 2020.

There are several outliers that immediately stand out. The river that
received the most pressure (Upper Missouri) isn't near a population
center and posted the largest increase in angling pressure from 2019 to
2020. On the contrary, the Bighorn is an outlier in the other direction,
experiencing a large decrease, possibly due to its relatively remote
location. This suggests that anglers favored rivers they could easily
drive to in a day.

\hypertarget{data-processing}{%
\subsubsection{Data Processing}\label{data-processing}}

One of the data processing challenges I had to navigate was removing
sections of river centerline that ran through lakes and reservoirs.
Fish, Wildlife \& Parks reports lake and stream pressure separately, so
leaving those sections in would have counted areas the pressure numbers
don't cover. I removed the sections intersecting lakes and reservoirs
and recalculated each river's length accordingly. The Upper Missouri
lost 51\% of its length to Canyon Ferry, Hauser, and Holter, moving its
value from 610 to 1,254 angling days per mile. The Lower Clark Fork lost
57\% to Noxon Reservoir and Cabinet Gorge.

To normalize the data, I joined the 2019 and 2020 stream pressure
metrics to the stream layer - appending the fields of one table to those
of another based on a common attribute - then calculated angling days
per river mile for each year, and added a third field taking the
difference between them.

One thing that's important to note about the angling pressure metric is
that it's reported by the entire drainage rather than the individual
water; therefore, I assigned each drainage's stream pressure to its main
stem - so tributary pressure is counted on the main stem as well.

    \hypertarget{discussion---second-iteration}{%
\subsection{Discussion - Second
Iteration}\label{discussion---second-iteration}}

When looking more closely at the data, you can see from the map and the
table/chart above that rivers with less pressure per mile tend to be
longer - which raises the question of whether this reflects a true
pattern or is simply a characteristic of river length itself. For
example, after lakes and reservoirs were trimmed from the Upper
Missouri, it lost 51\% of its length, shrinking the denominator of the
per-mile calculation and inflating the resulting metric. The Upper
Missouri showed the greatest increase of any river in this study,
despite being one of the shortest. This effect is clear when looking at
the gap between the Upper Missouri and the second-ranked Upper
Yellowstone, which more than doubles (1254.4 vs.~574.6), while the
remaining rivers show a relatively smooth decline in pressure.

Looking closely at the table, some of the longest rivers show very
little increase in angling pressure. I suspect this is because many of
these longer rivers run through the eastern part of the state, where
fisheries provide fewer angling opportunities and are generally less
desirable - some carry little to no trout and contain mostly warm-water
species. Typically, when we think of high-pressure rivers in Montana, we
picture trout streams; however, I will explore different normalization
methods below to see how the geospatial patterns might change.

\subsubsection{Discussion - Fourth
Iteration}\label{discussion---fourth-iteration}}

There is a strong negative correlation between 2019 pressure per access
point and the percent change from 2019 to 2020. The scatterplot above
shows that in general, rivers with lower 2019 pressure showed large
percentage increases in 2020. While this doesn't prove that more remote
rivers showed a large increase in angling pressure, it does provide
evidence that less popular rivers showed a large increase in angling
pressure. Setting the Bighorn River aside, the Gallatin and Madison
rivers were the most popular in 2019 and showed a relatively modest
increase in fishing pressure - further evidence that anglers sought out
less popular waters in 2020. The limitation of this study is sample size
- fourteen rivers is a small dataset, and a strong correlation drawn
from so few points should be viewed cautiously.

    \hypertarget{discussion---final-iteration}{%
\subsection{Discussion - Final
Iteration}\label{discussion---final-iteration}}

For the last iteration of this analysis, I am looking at raw total
stream pressure per drainage, with no normalizing metric applied. The
Tongue River drainage is the standout result in this view of the
dataset. This view highlights something the other normalization metrics
missed entirely. Despite being one of the longest rivers (211.7 miles)
with only one access point, the Tongue River showed a 122.6\% increase
--- from 10,626 days in 2019 to 23,653 days in 2020. This points to real
limitations in the previous iterations of this analysis. Also
noteworthy: the Upper Missouri River drainage shows a large increase
here too, one that can no longer be attributed to ratio sensitivity.
Perhaps aggregating raw total pressure at the drainage level, instead of
dividing by either length or access count, is the most telling display
of this data.

The other outlier worth addressing is the Bighorn River drainage, which
experienced a large decrease in angling pressure despite being a
renowned fishery. The Bighorn flows through the heart of the Crow Indian
Reservation, which enacted strict COVID-19 measures and discouraged
non-tribal member recreational travel. In late March 2020, the Crow
Tribe set up road checkpoints and issued a stay-at-home order
restricting non-essential travel onto the reservation
(\href{https://www.ktvq.com/news/coronavirus/crow-tribe-puts-up-road-side-checkpoints-to-curb-travel-to-reservation}{KTVQ,
April 1, 2020}). Around the same time, the National Park Service closed
its Bighorn River and Bighorn Reservoir boat ramps near Fort Smith at
the request of Big Horn County, the Bureau of Reclamation, and the Crow
Tribal Government
(\href{https://billingsgazette.com/lifestyles/recreation/bighorn-canyon-nra-closes-boat-ramps-on-bighorn-river-reservoir/article_71a7dc8c-de96-5059-bf44-3be6ebf91879.html}{Billings
Gazette, March 31, 2020}). It's reasonable to attribute the Bighorn's
decline in angling pressure to these access restrictions.

    \hypertarget{data-processing}{%
\subsection{Data Processing}\label{data-processing}}

To calculate percent change from the raw drainage-level pressure totals,
I used the formula (2020 pressure − 2019 pressure) / 2019 pressure ×
100. I calculated the 25th percentile of each drainage's 2019 baseline
pressure and excluded drainages below that threshold. This exclusion
omits drainages that had low baselines and were susceptible to a
distorted percent change, similar to the access point threshold I set
above.
